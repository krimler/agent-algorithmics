Notation and Conventions

You can skip this chapter and come back when a symbol surprises you. It is here so that when Chapter 9 writes \(B_t - R_t\) without comment, you know what both terms mean and why the subtraction matters.

Time and Execution

Time is discrete, indexed \(t = 1, 2, \ldots\). An agent runs in steps, and at each step it observes, decides, and possibly acts. Wall-clock time appears separately when latency is the subject; until then, a “step” is one turn through that loop.

State, Observation, and History

  • \(S_t\) denotes the environment state at time \(t\), which the agent generally cannot see.

  • \(o_t\) denotes what the agent actually observes at time \(t\).

  • \(H_t = (o_1, a_1, \ldots, o_t)\) denotes the execution history through time \(t\).

The distinction between \(S_t\) and \(o_t\) does real work in this book. Agents act on \(H_t\), a lossy and often stale record, while consequences land on \(S_t\).

Actions and Policies

  • \(a_t \in \mathcal{A}\) denotes the action taken at time \(t\).

  • \(\pi(\cdot \mid H_t)\) denotes the policy, mapping histories to actions.

Policies may be deterministic or stochastic, and may carry internal state of their own (a budget tracker, a memory index, a set of open commitments).

Cost and Budgets

  • \(c(e_t)\) denotes cost of event \(e_t\).

  • \(C(\tau)\) denotes total cost accumulated over a trace \(\tau\).

  • \(B_t\) denotes budget remaining at time \(t\).

  • \(R_t\) denotes budget reserved for work the agent has not done yet but must.

  • \(\hat{R}_t\) denotes the estimated cost of completing the task from the current state. It is an estimate, so it carries a distribution rather than a single number.

  • \(\Delta_t = B_t - \hat{R}_t\) denotes slack, the budget available after setting aside what finishing is expected to cost.

Cost covers tokens, latency, external resource consumption, and the expected cost of failure. Budgets are hard constraints unless a passage says otherwise. \(R_t\) is the term that separates an agent which finishes from one that runs out of money three steps from the answer.

Traces

A trace \(\tau = \langle e_1, \ldots, e_T \rangle\) is the sequence of execution events, namely observations, decisions, actions, gate outcomes, commits, and aborts.

Correctness, cost, and alignment are properties of traces. A single output can look impeccable in a trace that violated an invariant four steps earlier, which is why Chapter 14 treats the trace as the unit of evaluation.

Gates and Control Decisions

A gate is a control decision applied before commitment. Its outcomes are \[\{\textsc{Execute}, \textsc{Verify}, \textsc{Abstain}, \textsc{Refuse}\}.\]

Gates read risk estimates, budgets, policies, and evidence. Chapter 9 derives the thresholds rather than asserting them.

Speculation and Commitment

  • Speculation denotes tentative execution whose results may be thrown away.

  • Commitment denotes the moment results become irreversible and externally visible.

Speculative work is assumed abortable. Chapter 8 spends considerable effort on how often that assumption is false in practice, including the case where an aborted branch still influences the model through retained cache state.

Invariants and Policies

  • An invariant must hold at every step of execution.

  • A policy is a predicate constraining which actions are admissible.

Breaking an invariant counts as system failure even when the task succeeded. A system that refunds the right customer by writing directly to the ledger has still failed.

Conventions

  • Boldface marks defined terms on first use, and little else.

  • Probabilities and expectations condition on the agent’s available information unless stated otherwise.

  • Dollar figures and per-token prices are illustrative. Absolute prices move fast; the ratios between model tiers have been stable for years, and the arguments in this book depend only on the ratios.

  • Examples are illustrative rather than exhaustive.
